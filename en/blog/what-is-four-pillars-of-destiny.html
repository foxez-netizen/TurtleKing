What Is the Four Pillars of Destiny? A Beginner's Guide to Saju (Bazi)

Guide · 6 min read · Published 2026-10-08 · By Lucky Turtle Saju

If you have ever watched a Korean drama in which a character visits a fortune teller, or heard someone say a couple is "a perfect match by saju," you have already brushed against the Four Pillars of Destiny. In Korea the system is called saju (사주, 四柱, literally "four pillars") or palja (팔자, 八字, "eight characters"). In Chinese it is Bazi (八字), and in Japan it is known as shichusuimei. The three traditions share a common ancestor but have drifted apart in details, which we cover in our comparison of Korean and Chinese practice.

This guide explains, from scratch, what the Four Pillars are, how a chart is constructed, and how to think about the system sensibly. It is written for curious beginners, not for people looking for a verdict on their future.

The core idea in one paragraph

Saju treats the moment of your birth as a snapshot of the cycles of time: the year, the month, the day and the two-hour period of the day. Each of those four units is written as a pair of characters, one from a set of ten (the Heavenly Stems) and one from a set of twelve (the Earthly Branches). Four pairs make eight characters, and the relationships between those eight characters, interpreted through the Five Elements of Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water, are read as a description of temperament, strengths, tensions, and the rhythm of different life periods.

The four pillars

PillarTime unitTraditional association
Year pillarYear of birthAncestry, early childhood, the outer social environment
Month pillarMonth of birthParents, upbringing, career potential; the single most weighty pillar for judging the season you were born into
Day pillarDay of birthThe self (upper character) and the spouse or closest partner (lower character)
Hour pillarTwo-hour period of birthChildren, later life, aspirations

These associations are conventions of the tradition rather than empirical findings. They are useful mainly as a map for reading the chart: the same character can mean something different depending on which pillar it sits in.

Stems and branches: why each pillar has two characters

Every pillar has a top character and a bottom character. The top is a Heavenly Stem, one of ten: Jia, Yi, Bing, Ding, Wu, Ji, Geng, Xin, Ren and Gui (甲乙丙丁戊己庚辛壬癸). The bottom is an Earthly Branch, one of twelve: Zi, Chou, Yin, Mao, Chen, Si, Wu, Wei, Shen, You, Xu and Hai (子丑寅卯辰巳午未申酉戌亥). The twelve branches are also the twelve animals of the Chinese zodiac, from the Rat to the Pig. Stems and branches combine into a repeating cycle of 60 pairs, used since antiquity to count years, months, days and hours. We unpack that cycle in Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches.

The Day Master: the character that stands for "you"

The most important single character in the chart is the top character of the day pillar, called the Day Master (일간 in Korean, 日主 or 日干 in Chinese). The Day Master represents the self. The other seven characters are read in relation to it: some support it, some drain it, some challenge it. That relationship vocabulary is the system of Ten Gods, which assigns names such as "Friend," "Direct Wealth" or "Seven Killings" to every character in the chart.

The idea of centering the reading on the day rather than the year is traditionally credited to the Song dynasty scholar Xu Ziping, which is why modern Bazi is sometimes called the Ziping method. Earlier systems relied more heavily on the year pillar. You will see this history repeated in most sources; as with many origin stories in traditional divination, the details are hard to verify, so treat the attribution as the tradition's own account.

How a chart is actually built

Here is the part most beginner articles skip: a Saju chart is not built from the ordinary calendar. It uses the movement of the Sun.

  • The year changes at Lichun (立春, "Start of Spring"), around February 4, not on January 1 and not on Lunar New Year. In 2026 it occurred on February 4 at about 04:58 Korean time.
  • The month changes at twelve "solar term" boundaries (節, jie), such as Lichun, Jingzhe and Qingming, each about 30 degrees apart along the Sun's apparent path. A person born on May 3 therefore belongs to the month that began in early April, not to a "May" month. This post shows why that matters for your zodiac animal.
  • The day follows a continuous 60-day cycle that has run without interruption for centuries, so the day pillar of any date can be computed by counting.
  • The hour is one of twelve two-hour blocks, and the stem of the hour pillar is determined by the stem of the day.

Because of this, accurate software is needed to find the exact moment a solar term begins. Our own calculator does this astronomically, which we describe in the calculator review.

What a reading looks at

Once the eight characters are on the page, a practitioner typically asks a sequence of questions:

  1. What is the Day Master's element and polarity (for example, Yang Metal)?
  2. What season was it born in, and does the chart support or exhaust the Day Master?
  3. Which of the Five Elements are abundant, weak or absent?
  4. Which Ten Gods appear, in which pillars, and how strongly?
  5. Do any branches clash, combine or harm one another?
  6. How do the current ten-year luck cycle and annual pillar interact with the natal chart?

For a worked example of all six steps, see How to Read Your Saju Chart Step by Step.

What Saju is, and what it is not

It is worth being plain about this. Saju is a long-standing interpretive tradition, rich in symbolism and deeply embedded in Korean, Chinese and Japanese culture. It is a useful vocabulary for reflecting on temperament and for starting conversations. It is not a scientifically validated method of prediction, and we are not aware of controlled evidence that birth data determines personality or life events. Reputable practitioners themselves tend to say that the chart describes tendencies, and that choices, health, effort and circumstance matter more.

A healthy way to use it: read the chart as a mirror for self-reflection, the way you might read a personality framework, and never as a reason to avoid medical care, make a major financial decision, or end a relationship.

Common beginner questions

Do I need my exact birth time?

The year, month and day pillars can be built from the date alone, which already gives six of the eight characters. The hour pillar needs the time of birth, ideally within an hour or two. Without it you get a "three pillar" reading, which is common and still informative.

Does my birthplace matter?

Much less than in Western astrology. Saju uses the clock time of birth, and some Korean practitioners apply a longitude correction for Seoul, which we discuss in the Korean-versus-Chinese comparison. Houses and a rising sign, which depend heavily on location, have no direct equivalent here.

Is "Saju" the same as the Chinese zodiac?

No. The zodiac animal is only the branch of your birth year, one of eight characters. A full chart gives each of the four pillars its own animal, and the day and month animals are often considered more revealing than the year animal.
