Korean Saju vs Chinese Bazi: Key Differences in Calculation and Interpretation

Comparison · 5 min read · Published 2026-10-08 · By Lucky Turtle Saju

Saju, Bazi and shichusuimei all descend from the same Chinese system of four pillars and eight characters, so a chart for the same birth should look nearly identical across traditions. In practice, though, you will sometimes find two calculators that give you a different hour pillar, or even a different day pillar. This comparison explains the places where the traditions diverge, so you can understand why and decide which convention to trust.

What is the same

The skeleton is shared. All three use the 10 Heavenly Stems and 12 Earthly Branches, the year changing at Lichun, months changing at the twelve "jie" solar terms, the Day Master as the center of the reading, the Five Elements, and the Ten Gods. If you have learned from a Chinese textbook, you can read a Korean chart, and vice versa. The differences are in the edges.

1. The Zi hour and the date line problem

The first Earthly Branch, Zi (子, the Rat hour), runs from 23:00 to 01:00. That straddles midnight, so a baby born at 23:30 is in the Rat hour of which day? Two conventions exist:

  • Early Zi / "Yaja-si" (야자시, 夜子時): 23:00–24:00 belongs to the current day for the day pillar, while the hour pillar is still Zi. The next day begins at 00:00.
  • Late Zi / "Joja-si" (조자시, 早子時): the new day begins at 23:00, so a 23:30 birth takes the next day's day pillar.

Both appear in Chinese and Korean practice, and sources disagree about which is more orthodox. The choice changes the day pillar, and with it the Day Master, so it is the single most consequential setting. A good calculator lets you choose; ours offers both in advanced settings, and in the Korean community the early-Zi convention is common, though not universal. If your birth time falls between 23:00 and midnight, compare both versions and ask which one feels more coherent.

2. Which clock? Time zones and longitude

Korean Standard Time is UTC+9, set to the meridian at 135°E, which passes through Japan. Seoul sits at about 127°E, so true solar time in Seoul runs roughly 30 minutes behind the clock. Many Korean practitioners therefore subtract about 30 minutes from the birth time before choosing the hour pillar, to approximate local solar time. Others use the clock time as written on the birth record. The correction rarely changes anything, but it can move a birth across a two-hour boundary: 14:45 clock time becomes 14:15 true time, still the Wei hour, but 13:10 would move from the Wei hour back into the Wu hour.

Chinese Bazi practice has a similar debate, since China uses a single time zone (UTC+8) across a vast territory, and serious practitioners often adjust for the birth city's longitude, which can mean differences of several hours in far western regions. Korea's single correction (about minus 30 minutes for Seoul) is a smaller version of the same idea.

Historical time also complicates things. South Korea used UTC+8:30 between 1954 and 1961, and observed daylight saving time in some years including 1987 and 1988, so the clock reading on an older birth record may not be the same as standard time. If you were born during such a period, ask a Korean almanac or a practitioner to convert the time. Our calculator, like most, treats the time you enter as standard Korean time (UTC+9) and does not apply daylight-saving corrections on its own.

3. Lunar calendars and conversion

Saju does not rely on the lunar calendar for its pillars, which use the solar terms. But many Koreans know their birthday as a lunar date, so calculators must convert. Korea and China each define the lunar calendar from astronomical new moons, but the time zone used (UTC+9 versus UTC+8) can occasionally put a new moon on different calendar days. Therefore a lunar birthday converted to a solar date can differ by a day between Korean and Chinese sources in rare cases, especially for older dates. Leap months can also be placed differently when historical conventions varied. If your only birth record is lunar, double-check it against an official Korean lunar-solar converter.

4. Symbolic stars (sinsal) and what gets emphasized

Classical Chinese texts emphasize the structure of the chart: strength, season, useful element. Korean practice tends to give more attention to a family of "symbolic stars" called sinsal (신살, 神煞). Popular ones include:

  • Dohwa-sal (도화살, "peach blossom"): charm and romantic attraction.
  • Yeokma-sal (역마살, "post-horse"): movement, travel, relocation. Considered favorable in a globalized era.
  • Hwagae-sal (화개살, "flower canopy"): artistic, spiritual or scholarly inclination.
  • Baekho-daesal (백호대살, "white tiger"): intensity and accident-proneness in older texts.

The star names exist in Chinese texts as well, but Korean YouTube explainers, fortune-telling shops, and apps lean on them heavily, which is partly why a Korean reading can feel more colorful and dramatic. Many Chinese Bazi teachers consider stars secondary to structure. Rules for each star vary by source; our calculator uses the criteria most widely used in Korea.

6. How the system is used

In Korea Saju has a strong association with gunghap (궁합, couple compatibility), selecting auspicious wedding dates, naming babies, and New Year readings. In China Bazi is more often tied to career and wealth analysis, and in Japan shichusuimei is often studied alongside other divination. For more on Korea specifically, read Saju in Korean Culture.

So which one is correct?

None of these conventions is "wrong." They reflect different choices that predate computers. The practical advice:

  • If your birth time is between 23:00 and 01:00 or close to a two-hour boundary, generate charts under both conventions and compare.
  • If your birth date is within a day of a solar term (the 4th through 8th of most months), look up the exact term time in a reliable almanac.
  • Stick to one calculator and one method when comparing over time, so you are reading a consistent chart.
  • Remember that the interpretation, not the arithmetic, is where the schools truly diverge.
