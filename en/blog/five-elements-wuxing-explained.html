The Five Elements (Wuxing) Explained: Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water in Saju

Guide · 5 min read · Published 2026-10-08 · By Lucky Turtle Saju

The Five Elements are the working vocabulary of nearly every East Asian system of correlative thinking, from Chinese medicine to feng shui to the Four Pillars of Destiny. In Chinese they are called Wuxing (五行), in Korean Ohaeng (오행). The English word "elements" is a slightly misleading translation: the Chinese term xing means something closer to "phases" or "agents," meaning processes that move into one another, rather than fixed substances like the elements of Greek philosophy.

In Saju, every one of the eight characters in your chart belongs to one of these five phases. Understanding them is the single most useful step before reading anything else about your chart.

The five, and what each one stands for

The color-coding used on this site follows a common convention: green for Wood, red for Fire, yellow for Earth, white or silver for Metal, and dark blue or black for Water. These colors are a memory aid, not part of the calculation.

Notice that Earth is the odd one out. In the simple four-season picture it gets no season of its own, so it is assigned to the roughly eighteen days before each seasonal turn, and it also acts as the "center" that holds the other four together. That is why two of the twelve Earthly Branches (Chen and Xu) sit at season changes and carry Earth, along with Chou and Wei.

Yin and Yang inside each element

Each element comes in a Yang form and a Yin form, which is how ten Heavenly Stems arise from five elements. Yang is outward, bold, large-scale; Yin is inward, refined, small-scale. This is why Yang Wood (Jia) is traditionally pictured as a tall tree and Yin Wood (Yi) as grass, vines or flowers.

These images are a mnemonic that practitioners use to give each character a personality at a glance. They are interpretive traditions, and different schools phrase them differently.

The generating cycle: who nourishes whom

In the generating (or "productive") cycle, each element gives rise to the next, like a mother feeding a child:

Wood feeds Fire, Fire creates Earth (ash), Earth bears Metal (ore), Metal gathers Water (condensation), Water nourishes Wood.

The element that generates you is your "resource" or "mother"; the element you generate is your "output" or "child." This mother-and-child language reappears in the Ten Gods, where "Resource" and "Output" are two of the five families.

The controlling cycle: who restrains whom

In the controlling cycle, each element restrains another one two steps along the generating sequence:

Wood parts Earth, Earth dams Water, Water extinguishes Fire, Fire melts Metal, Metal cuts Wood.

Control is not purely negative. A river with no banks floods; a tree with no axe becomes unruly. Practitioners often describe healthy control as discipline or structure, and describe a total absence of it as lacking shape, or a total excess as oppression.

Excess, deficiency and the idea of balance

The point of counting elements is to see which are abundant and which are thin. A few classic patterns:

  • Too much of one element can be read as a strong, sometimes one-sided temperament. Lots of Fire is imagined as passionate and quick to burn out; lots of Water as thoughtful but diffuse.
  • A missing element is read as an area that takes conscious effort rather than a defect. In popular Korean usage you will often hear "your chart lacks Wood," usually followed by a harmless suggestion about colors or hobbies.
  • A strong element beside its controller can be moderated; a strong element that is also generated by a neighbor compounds.

The tradition does not say that a balanced chart is a happy chart, nor that an unbalanced one is doomed. Many historically accomplished people are described by practitioners as having extremely lopsided charts, which is itself taken as a source of distinctiveness. Treat any claim that an element deficit "causes" a problem with skepticism.

How elements are counted in a real chart

A first-pass count simply looks at all eight visible characters: four stems and four branches. Each branch has a fixed element, as do the stems.

More detailed counts also include the hidden stems (藏干) tucked inside each branch. For example, the branch Si (巳), though counted as Fire, contains hidden Earth and Metal; Wei (未) contains Earth, Fire and Wood. Weighted counts like this are what the bar chart on our calculator reports, and they matter because a chart that "has no Metal" on the surface may have some hiding underground.

Finally, the season modifies the count. An element is strongest in its own season and in the season just before it. A Fire character born in summer is considered much stronger than the same character born in winter. This is why the month pillar is so influential.

Where else the Five Elements appear

The same five-fold scheme organizes directions (Wood east, Fire south, Metal west, Water north, Earth center), organs in traditional Chinese medicine, flavors, planets and more. Korean culture still carries visible traces: the five colors of obangsaek in traditional dress and architecture, and the multicolored toppings of bibimbap are often cited as examples of the same cosmology at work in daily life.

These correspondences are cultural associations built up over two thousand years. They are best understood as a symbolic filing system, a way of organizing observations, rather than as literal physical laws.

Takeaways

  • Think of the Five Elements as phases of a cycle, not as five kinds of material.
  • Remember two loops: the generating cycle (Wood → Fire → Earth → Metal → Water → Wood) and the controlling cycle (Wood → Earth → Water → Fire → Metal → Wood).
  • Count all eight characters, then the hidden stems, then check the season.
  • Read an imbalance as a tendency to be aware of, not as a prediction.
