Saju vs MBTI: Why Korea Loves Both and How They Compare

Comparison · 4 min read · Published 2026-10-08 · By Lucky Turtle Saju

Ask a young person in Seoul to introduce themselves and you may hear four letters before a hobby: "I'm an ENFP." Ask the same person about a partner and they might mention a Saju compatibility reading, or gunghap. In Korea, the 16-type MBTI and the thousand-year-old Four Pillars sit side by side as competing and complementary ways to talk about personality. This comparison looks at how they are built, what each can legitimately claim, and how to enjoy them without being misled.

What is being measured?

MBTI is a self-report instrument. It tells you how you answered, which is a reflection of how you see yourself on the day you take it. That is both its strength (people recognize themselves) and its weakness (people are not reliable narrators of their own traits, and moods affect answers).

Saju is a calendar-derived chart. It involves no questions, so it cannot be influenced by what you say. The flip side is that nothing in the method has been shown to link birth moments to personality: the chart is an interpretive system applied to a date, not a measurement of the person.

Reliability and validity

For MBTI, the psychology literature is critical but nuanced. Researchers have repeatedly pointed out that the four scales are treated as categories (you are either "E" or "I") even though the underlying scores are continuous and bunched near the middle, so people near the boundary can flip type on retesting. Studies have reported that a substantial share of people receive a different type when retested after several weeks. Personality psychologists generally prefer the Big Five model (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism), which has stronger statistical support. MBTI remains popular partly because it is positive, non-judgmental and easy to discuss.

For Saju, as in our comparison with Western astrology, we are not aware of large controlled studies demonstrating predictive validity. It is better understood as a cultural framework than as a psychometric test.

Why the combination thrives in Korea

MBTI exploded in Korean popular culture in the late 2010s and early 2020s, appearing in dating profiles, job interviews' small talk, variety shows, and even marketing campaigns. Commentators have offered several explanations: a culture that already enjoyed categorizing people by birth-year animals and blood type, a desire for a quick shorthand in a fast-moving social world, and the fact that the type labels are flattering and shareable. Saju, meanwhile, had never gone away: it moved from fortune-teller shops into apps, online calculators and "Saju cafés."

People increasingly use them together. A common conversation goes: "My MBTI says I'm a planner, and my Saju says I have a lot of Wood, so I like to grow things and make plans." It is playful narrative-making, not rigorous analysis. For the cultural background, see Saju in Korean Culture.

Strengths and weaknesses side by side

MBTI

  • Strengths: Quick, language everyone knows, encourages talking about differences in preference (for example, introvert versus extrovert needs).
  • Weaknesses: Binary categories hide gradations, results vary, and some employers misuse it for hiring, which the Myers-Briggs organization itself discourages.

Saju

  • Strengths: Rich symbolic vocabulary, a built-in time dimension, a calming focus on balance rather than types, and deep cultural context.
  • Weaknesses: Schools disagree on rules, interpretations can be fatalistic if misused, and the claims are not empirically supported.

A sensible way to use both

  1. Treat them as prompts, not labels. A description that resonates is an invitation to reflect, not a diagnosis.
  2. Notice contradictions. If MBTI says "highly structured" and your chart says "free-flowing," that mismatch is itself interesting to think about, and a reminder that both are partial.
  3. Prefer behavior over type. What you do repeatedly matters more than any four letters or eight characters.
  4. Never use either to judge other people. Writing off a coworker or partner because of an MBTI type or a Day Master is the same mistake in two languages.

Review verdict

If you want a conversation starter built on your own answers, MBTI is accessible and widely understood, but the evidence suggests the Big Five is a sounder tool for actual self-knowledge. If you want a framework that places you in a pattern of time and elements, Saju offers something MBTI cannot: a long-running cultural narrative about seasons, balance and cycles.
