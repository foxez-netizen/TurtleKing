Saju in Korean Culture: Fortune Cafés, Gunghap and New Year Readings

Visit Seoul on a weekend afternoon, and somewhere between the street food and the boutiques you may notice small signs advertising saju and tarot in the same breath. Pass a fortune-telling café near a university and you might see a queue of students in their twenties. For many Koreans, saju (사주) is neither a deep religious practice nor a joke: it sits somewhere in between, as a social ritual, a conversation topic, and for some a serious source of guidance. This post sketches where it fits in Korean life, as cultural context for readers meeting the Four Pillars for the first time.

A long history of reading time

The Four Pillars system arrived in Korea from China along with the broader package of calendrical and cosmological ideas (Yin-Yang, the Five Elements, the sexagenary cycle) that shaped Korean statecraft and daily life for centuries. During the Joseon dynasty (1392–1897), the court maintained an official bureau for astronomy, calendars and related matters, known as the Gwansanggam (관상감), and almanac-making was a matter of state authority: a calendar was a sign of legitimate rule. Royal and aristocratic families consulted specialists for auspicious dates, a practice called taekil (택일).

The vocabulary also seeped into ordinary language. The word palja (팔자), "eight characters," is a common everyday word for "fate" or "lot in life." A Korean might sigh "palja-ga sega-nada" (팔자가 세다, "I have a tough fate") or say that someone "palja-ga joda" (팔자가 좋다, "has a good fate") without ever having had their chart read.

Gunghap: compatibility before marriage

The best-known use of saju in family life is gunghap (궁합), the comparison of two people's charts for compatibility. Traditionally, families consulted a specialist before a marriage was agreed, comparing the couple's year and day pillars and looking for harmonious or conflicting elements. Today, many couples treat gunghap as a bit of fun, a mixture of romance and curiosity, though some families, especially older generations, still take it seriously. Online gunghap tests are everywhere, often combined with MBTI compatibility and blood-type charts. See our Saju versus MBTI comparison.

A few typical elements of a gunghap-style reading: whether the two year animals clash or combine (for example, Rat and Horse are traditionally opposite), whether the Day Masters support each other in the Five Element cycles, and whether one partner's chart contains elements that the other lacks. Modern practitioners generally frame it as "complementary" or "needs effort" rather than "good" or "bad."

Baby names and auspicious dates

Parents-to-be sometimes visit a specialist to choose a name (jakmyeong, 작명) that supplies elements missing from a baby's chart, or pay for an auspicious date for a cesarean birth, a wedding, a housewarming or the opening of a business. The practice has attracted criticism, particularly where families feel pressured to schedule births by the chart, but it persists as a cultural habit. Many modern Korean parents treat name selection as a combination of family tradition, aesthetics and a nod to the chart.

New Year readings: tojeong bigyeol

Around Lunar New Year it is common to get a reading of the year's fortune. A popular traditional form is the Tojeong Bigyeol (토정비결), a text of year-by-year fortunes traditionally attributed to Yi Ji-ham, a 16th-century scholar who used the pen name Tojeong. It determines your fortune from your birth date and the current year using a numerical scheme rather than the full Four Pillars chart. Alongside it, many people consult saju specialists, apps, or online tools for the year ahead. Mobile apps push out "this year's fortune" notifications every January and February.

The modern scene: cafés, apps and online classes

In the past decade saju moved from the back-alley fortune-teller's room to polished storefronts, cafés where you order a drink and receive a short reading, pop-up booths at festivals, and a large number of subscription apps and websites. YouTube channels teach saju to a mass audience, including long series that analyze historical figures' charts. Surveys in Korea have suggested that a notable share of young adults have had their fortune told in some form, and the topic regularly appears in dramas, variety shows and webtoons. Exact numbers vary by survey and should be treated as approximate.

The audience tends to be pragmatic. Many young people describe saju as a way to feel less alone with uncertainty, especially around job-hunting, relationships and big decisions in a competitive society. Others emphasize it as a fun conversation starter, similar to a horoscope.

Respect, skepticism and religion

Korea is a plural religious society, and attitudes vary. Some Christian denominations discourage fortune-telling; some Buddhists consult monks and almanacs; Korean shamanism (musok) is a distinct tradition involving spirit mediums and rituals, and should not be confused with chart-based saju, which is a calendrical and astrological method. Many Koreans are openly skeptical, and the common attitude is something like: "If it says something good, I'll believe it; if not, I'll ignore it." That half-joking line captures how lightly many people hold it.

A note on ethics and consumer protection

As with any industry that sells hope, there are bad actors. Consumer groups in Korea and elsewhere caution against fortune tellers who claim a "curse" or demand large payments for rituals. A trustworthy practitioner states limits, avoids fatalistic pronouncements, and never pressures you to spend money or to make irreversible choices. The same advice applies to online content. If a reading frightens you, close it and talk to someone you trust.

If you are visiting Korea

  • Fortune cafés and booths usually offer short readings; some provide English-speaking readers or interpreters, but quality varies.
  • Ask the price in advance, and treat the reading as entertainment.

Saju's place in Korean culture is best seen as a shared vocabulary: a way of talking about luck, timing, and temperament that families and friends already understand. Whether or not you believe in it, understanding it helps you understand a lot of Korean conversation.
