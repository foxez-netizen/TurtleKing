Four Pillars (Saju) vs Western Astrology: How They Differ and What They Share

Comparison · 4 min read · Published 2026-10-08 · By Lucky Turtle Saju

If you grew up in the United States or Europe, you probably know your sun sign. If you grew up in Korea, you probably know your Chinese zodiac animal and, increasingly, your MBTI type. Both horoscope traditions turn the moment of birth into a symbolic map. But they work very differently, and comparing them clarifies what each one really does. This is a comparison and review of the two systems as frameworks, not a ranking of which is "right."

Both are anchored to the Sun, in different ways

The two systems are less alien to each other than they first appear. Saju's month boundaries are the "solar terms," points on the Sun's yearly path 30 degrees apart, beginning with Lichun at 315 degrees of ecliptic longitude. Western signs are also 30-degree slices of the Sun's path, but they begin at the spring equinox (0 degrees, the start of Aries). The two grids are offset by 15 degrees, roughly two weeks: Saju's "Tiger month" begins around February 4, whereas Aquarius begins around January 20 and Pisces around February 19. That is why your Western sun sign and your Saju month branch rarely line up.

A direct consequence: in Saju the "year" does not begin on January 1 or Lunar New Year but at Lichun. For how that changes your zodiac animal, see this guide.

Location matters differently

Western astrology requires your birthplace to compute the Ascendant (Rising sign) and the houses, which can change within minutes as the sky rotates. This is why astrologers insist on the exact time and city. Saju needs only the date and the clock time, though some Korean practitioners also apply a longitude correction, and the hour pillar has two-hour granularity, so small time errors matter far less. This is a real practical difference: someone with an uncertain birth time can get a useful Saju chart of six characters, while a Rising sign would be a guess.

The kind of story each tells

Western astrology tends to describe psychology and narrative: a Moon in Cancer feels deeply, a Mars square Saturn struggles with frustration. The chart reads like a cast of characters with relationships among them.

Saju tends to describe balance and flow: which elements are strong or weak, whether the Day Master is supported, and which period of time brings which energy. The chart reads more like an ecosystem across seasons, and the luck cycles give it a built-in timeline, which Western astrology achieves through transits and progressions.

Both give you an interpretive vocabulary. Neither vocabulary is arbitrary: each is a coherent symbolic language developed by many generations of readers, which is part of why people find them satisfying.

What does the evidence say?

This is the part sources often skip. For Western astrology, there has been actual testing. A widely cited double-blind study by Shawn Carlson, published in Nature in 1985, asked professional astrologers to match natal charts to personality profiles and found performance at the level of chance. Later reviews have been broadly consistent: sun-sign descriptions have not been shown to predict personality or events better than chance.

For Saju and Bazi, we are not aware of equivalent large controlled studies in the peer-reviewed literature. That is not evidence that it works; it is absence of rigorous testing. The honest summary is that neither system has demonstrated predictive validity, and both are best treated as cultural and reflective traditions.

Why do the readings feel accurate then? Psychologists point to the Barnum effect, the tendency to accept vague, flattering descriptions as personally specific, and to confirmation bias, where we remember hits and forget misses. Both traditions are equally vulnerable. Being aware of this does not require you to dislike either system.

Which should you use?

If the question is "which is more useful," the answer depends on what you want from it:

  • Want a rich character vocabulary and a social ritual? Western astrology has an enormous English-language community, apps, and shared jargon that make it easy to talk about with friends.
  • Want a framework about balance, seasons and timing? Saju offers a structural approach that many people find fresh, particularly the idea that your chart is a pattern of elements to be balanced rather than a fixed personality label.
  • Want compatibility talk? Both have it: synastry in the West, and gunghap (궁합) in Korea, where it traditionally accompanied marriage decisions.
  • Want something predictive and reliable? Neither is. Use medical, financial and legal professionals for those questions.

Our review verdict

As frameworks for reflection, we find Saju's Five Elements balance model a more natural fit for discussing change over time, and Western astrology's psychological language richer for describing inner personality. As predictive tools, neither earns confidence. We run a Saju site, so take that preference with an appropriate grain of salt; a good test of any source is whether it openly states its limits.
